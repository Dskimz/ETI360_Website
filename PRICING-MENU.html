Service menu and pricing

Built from your confirmed prices and the effort bands recorded per document in the catalogue. Reviewed by Dan 26 August: the numbers are directionally right, the working ballpark is about $750 per reviewed trip, and the fine pass happens with Seb. Internal only, never customer-facing.

How to read this

Confirmed is a price you set. Proposed is mine, derived from the catalogue's effort band for that document. Open needs your decision.

The scale your prices imply

DOCUMENT-CATALOGUE-PROPOSAL.md assigns every document an effort band. Your numbers calibrate that scale inside a trip: the Itinerary Report is banded Medium and the Calendar View Low, and together you priced them at $100. The Weather Brief is banded Low to Medium at $25. That gives a working ladder of Low $25 · Low-Medium $40 · Medium $75 · High $150 per trip document. Tier 1 sits on its own scale because it is an annual engagement rather than a document.

The commercial consequence worth noticing first

This pricing model solves the funnel problem we have been working around for three rounds. When the entry price was a single $3,000 session, we needed a free offer to get a school into a conversation, and that free offer was an uncosted delivery obligation. At $25 for a weather brief and $325 for an essential trip pack, the first sale is the demonstration. A school can buy one trip's documents for the price of a textbook order, judge the work, and come back. No free work is needed anywhere.

The Duty Manager Simulation

Corrected description, per your note

The Simulation is not an entry product and does not stand alone. It runs after a school's trips are in the system: the itineraries built, the SOPs researched, the school's RAMS added, and the parent and public-facing documents produced. Only then can it run, because it uses the school's real trip data against ETI360's event factors, and the duty manager works a timeline of those real trips on the Duty Manager Dashboard, managing events as they arrive, to stress test the school's own procedures and the team's ability to run them.

Commercially this makes it the natural second sale to an existing document client, not the first sale to a stranger. The document work pays for itself and creates the conditions the Simulation needs.

Two things to go in with your eyes open

These prices sit well below the business plan's hypotheses. That plan assumed $1,500 to $2,500 per trip pack and $10,000 to $20,000 annual programs. Your menu lands a full trip review at roughly a fifth of that. It is a defensible choice and it buys much easier first sales, but it means the annual number per school is around $7,500 rather than $15,000, so the count of schools has to carry the revenue instead of the price.

The margin question is settled by the volume thesis. Ruled by Dan, 26 August: the first trip is not the win; a school with 50 trips at $750 is where the money is. So the first trip is knowingly thin, the baseline is knowingly cheap, and the account economics are judged on the program: fifty trips is $37,500 a year from one school, renewed through the annual baseline and the next calendar. The re-run engine is what makes trip thirty as profitable as trip three.

What this changes in the launch plan

  • The ladder inverts. Documents first, Simulation later. The emails now invite a conversation about an upcoming trip and its documents, not a $3,000 session.
  • No free work is needed. The entry price is low enough to be the demonstration, which retires the last argument for a free offer.
  • The briefing keeps its job but changes its content: it walks the menu against the school's own trip calendar and produces a quote, rather than scoping a single session.
  • Nothing here goes on a customer surface without your word. The standing rule keeps pricing off public pages; a menu like this is a document you send or walk through, not a page you publish.
